Riverty's banking licence and Mastercard platform are already in place, and a consumer card is the next product that can earn that investment.

## Cold-read your own hook
- **Is the business risk high enough to justify a new initiative, a high-stakes threat, or a minor inconvenience?:** It justifies a new initiative. It is not a high-stakes threat, and it is not a minor inconvenience.

Riverty's problem is a slow, compounding loss of ground. Brand preference is about 6%, growth rests on a single engine, and Klarna and PayPal keep building the consumer relationship Riverty lacks. The platform and licence investment is already made, so waiting costs money without any return. That is enough to act on.

It doesn't threaten the business. Merchants aren't leaving, and the modelled card economics are modest. Interchange on EUR 125m of year-1 volume is small by my rough estimate, so the case rests on interest from revolving balances. If the business case collapsed, the core business would be fine. That is why the right response is a lean, staged launch with clear stop criteria, not a large bet.

So my answer is: do it, keep it small, and make it prove adoption before it gets more capacity.
- **Is the moment of misery a systemic problem or just an edge case?:** It's systemic. The core of it comes from how Riverty's model is built, not from rare bad luck.

The in-store gap affects every user. Riverty isn't available at physical points of sale at all, so any user who shops in a store hits it.
Weak brand pull is structural. Lena uses whichever option appears at checkout, so Riverty is chosen by default, not preference. That describes the typical user, not an outlier.
Heavy users are capped by design. They can only use Riverty where partners offer it, and that limit follows directly from the merchant-first model.
Lena isn't an edge case. The brief describes her using BNPL on most non-trivial purchases with several apps installed, so her pattern is the target behavior.
- **Does the value proposition actually remove the obstacle you identified, or is it a generic feature?:** Mostly generic. It removes one obstacle, the in-store gap, but it doesn't yet give Lena a reason to choose Riverty over Klarna or PayPal.

What it does remove

A card that works anywhere solves the in-store problem directly, since Riverty is currently absent at physical tills. That part is real.

Where it falls short

"Pay later anywhere" is parity, not an edge. PayPal already offers a virtual card for instalments in stores, and Klarna's card is growing, so this makes Riverty comparable, not preferred.
It doesn't fix weak brand pull. The 6% brand preference means Lena picks whatever appears at checkout. The proposition says nothing that would make her choose Riverty when other options are on her phone.
"One clear view of what's due" only covers Riverty purchases. Lena juggles several apps, so a Riverty dashboard doesn't reduce the juggling unless she moves most of her spending onto the card, and I've given no reason she would.
"Decide first, pay after" depends on the product design. If the card is debit-first, as Klarna's is in Germany, the money leaves her account straight away, and she loses the very behavior she values.
The merchant benefit is an assumption. Nothing in the brief shows that the card sends customers toward partner merchants.
