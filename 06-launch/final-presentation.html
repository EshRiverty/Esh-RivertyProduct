Slide 5 · Strategy

From replaceable checkout button to everyday card

Problem hook

Customers like Lena juggle BNPL apps and pay in stores with someone else’s card, and with only 6% brand preference, each month Riverty waits to launch a card lets Klarna and PayPal tighten their hold on her and leaves Riverty a replaceable checkout button.

Value proposition

For busy BNPL users like Lena, who want money to leave their account only after they’ve decided to keep what they bought, the Riverty card lets them pay later anywhere, in stores and online, with one clear view of what’s due. Unlike Klarna’s debit-first card or PayPal’s separate instalment products, it keeps the “decide first, pay after” habit in every setting and sends users back to Riverty’s partner merchants instead of away from them.

Why now

  • Klarna’s card base is growing, and PayPal now offers instalments at Amazon.de and a virtual card for in-store payments, so Lena’s habits are being set by competitors.
  • From 20 November 2026, most BNPL falls under consumer credit rules, and Riverty carries that compliance cost with or without a card.
  • Riverty’s banking licence and Mastercard platform are already in place, and a consumer card is the next product that can earn that investment.

Data-backed hypothesis

If heavy German Riverty users who also hold PayPal or Klarna can use one Riverty card to pay later at any online shop, with all their card spending categorized in a single overview, then at least 50% of activated cardholders will make a second card purchase within 30 days, because today they use Riverty only where a merchant happens to offer it, and the card makes it available wherever they already shop. We will know we’re wrong if activation falls below about 50% or if integrated checkout volume per heavy user at partner merchants drops by more than about 5% against a matched group.

70.5%of Riverty users in Germany (30.4% strongly, 40.1% somewhat) agree that managing multiple payment systems makes it harder to keep track of their spending. Norstat survey, n=1,022

VerdictIt justifies a new initiative. It is not a high-stakes threat, and it is not a minor inconvenience.

Cold read: it removes one obstacle, the in-store gap, but it doesn’t yet give Lena a reason to choose Riverty over Klarna or PayPal.

Slide 6 · Research

What she does today instead, and where it breaks

Role

Salaried Germans aged 25 to 40, like Lena, who use BNPL on most non-trivial purchases, hold two or more pay-later providers, and at least sometimes want to pay later in a store.

Goal

To buy what they want, online or in a store, and have money leave their account only for what they’ve decided to keep, without having to track it all by hand.

Friction

The user has to run their own system to keep control. Nothing gives them a single view of what’s due, so the control they want depends on manual tracking they can’t fully trust.

Workaround: tools and process

  • A personal calendar for every due date, because the apps’ reminders aren’t trusted.
  • A debit card at the till when pay-later isn’t offered, which loses the deferral.
  • Competitor apps in parallel: Klarna and PayPal alongside Riverty (74% use two or more providers, synthetic survey).

The 5-step process

  1. Accept whatever option appears at checkout (68% in the synthetic survey).
  2. Find the due date in that provider’s app (inferred).
  3. Copy the date into a personal calendar (documented, P6).
  4. Repeat for every purchase and every provider (inferred).
  5. Check the calendar against the return window (inferred).

Why she is forced into it: no single product shows what’s due across providers, and no provider offers pay-later at the till.

Competitive landscape

  • Klarna’s card grew from 5 million users in March to 6.5 million active users by Q2.
  • PayPal gives approved users a virtual card for contactless instalment payments at the till, and is bringing instalments to Amazon.de with terms up to 48 months.
  • Instalment card banks keep collecting the interest income the card business case depends on.

Evidence synthetic

“I put every due date in my calendar because I can’t trust three apps to remind me properly.” P6, 31, Stuttgart

PRD highlights

Vision: Heavy online shoppers never have to remember what is due on their Riverty Card, and can settle it in one tap at the moment they decide to.

Must-haves

  • Scheduled reminder per card invoice, with merchant, amount and due date.
  • Deep link to that specific invoice.
  • One-tap Pay now, pre-filled from the linked bank account.
  • Reminder state in sync with payment, so a paid invoice stops reminding.

Demoted: postpone from the reminder. Made explicit: reminders cover card invoices only, which protects the integrated-checkout guardrail (a 1.54% merchant fee becomes 0.1% interchange, taking contribution margin from 1.3% to -0.1%).

Gaps the prototype exposed in the PRD

  • Dead end when no reminder is sent (notifications off or merchant missing).
  • Speed versus safety when the amount changes after the reminder.
  • The 2-action target is ambiguous: tapping the reminder body takes 3.
  • Reminder copy goes stale after delivery.

Slide 8 · Validation

A/B experiment: one-tap “Pay now” in the reminder

Hypothesis

I believe that a one-tap “Pay now” action in the due-date reminder for activated heavy German Riverty cardholders (five or more purchases a year) who shop mostly online will result in more users paying their card invoice shortly after the reminder, with fewer taps, as measured by a +10 percentage points change in the share of users whose first card invoice is paid within 24 hours of the reminder being delivered within 4 weeks, two full 14-day invoice cycles. We will protect integrated checkout volume per heavy user at partner merchants throughout the test.

Slide 9 · Launch

GTM: Tier M, owned-first, nothing before the A/B read

Goal · audience · tier

GoalAwareness: activated cardholders knowing reminders exist, keeping notifications switched on, and recognizing the Pay now action.

AudienceActivated heavy German Riverty cardholders (five or more purchases a year) who shop mostly online.

Tier MTargeted. Justified, but only just: about 21,900 reminded users in year 1 (assuming 70% opt-in), and roughly EUR 4 of interchange per card per year at 0.1%, so about EUR 1,250 a year if reminders lift repeat use by one point.

Channels

OwnedIn-app and email: a prompt to switch on reminders at card activation, a card-tab banner, a short email, a help-center article and release notes.

EarnedRide the card’s launch story, not a standalone push; pilot-user quotes with consent.

PaidNone recommended. The audience is reachable for free.

Enablement · owners · budget

German and English copy approved by legal and risk, three PRD screens in real UI, support FAQ and macros, a merchant success note, and reminder events live before any announcement. Experiment hygiene: keep Pay now out of all messaging until the A/B read.

Owners: product manager, product marketing, CRM owner, design, legal and risk, support lead, analytics, engineering. Budget capped at €100k

Bad signal to watch for

  • Opt-outs rising 2 or more points above the control group, or opt-in stalling because of the permission prompt.
  • High opens but few Pay now taps (aware but not acting).
  • Support contacts saying reminders felt like pressure, or asking about credit or SCHUFA effects.
  • Control-arm users seeing Pay now messaging.
  • Integrated checkout volume more than 5% below a matched group for two weekly checks.

Likely post-launch decision

Hold the broad announcement until the A/B read. If the ship criteria are met, announce to all activated cardholders and shift the goal from awareness to adoption. Low opt-in: fix the prompt first. Awareness high but action low: iterate on copy and lead time. Inconclusive: extend the test. Kill: keep reminders without Pay now.

Slide 10 · Story

What got in the way, what I learned, what comes next

Friction points along the way

  • Synthetic research. My first user research was synthetic. The real 2023 interviews ranked cashback first and showed most users prefer separate invoices to one consolidated due date.
  • AI smoothed over the critical frustration. The due-date scramble became a generic bullet, because severity was rated by business exposure, not user harm.
  • The first primary metric rewarded early payment. Paying within 24 hours measures speed, not the moment of misery, and the reminder arrives 3 days before the due date.
  • The prototype found PRD gaps that the document review had missed.

Aha moment

A reminder only works for users who allowed notifications.

In the prototype, the notifications-off scenario showed an empty lock screen with nothing to tap. For those users the feature does nothing, so opt-in, not reminder copy, is the first product risk. It is also why the launch plan leads with opt-in.

Key takeaways

  • Start from the moment of misery, not the feature list.
  • Label every number as real, synthetic or placeholder.
  • Audit AI scoring for user harm, not only business impact.
  • A prototype is the cheapest way to find PRD bugs.

What I would do next

  1. Measure the real baselines: late-or-missed invoice rate and notification opt-in.
  2. Fix the PRD gaps: an in-app route to unpaid invoices, a rule for changed amounts, and a defined 2-action path.
  3. Re-run the A/B on the missed-due-date rate, with refund-after-payment and opt-outs as guardrails.
  4. Ask for notification permission at the first invoice, name the owners, and set a spend ceiling below the €100k cap.
  5. Decide on consumer credit after the pilot, as an optional-instalment test.
